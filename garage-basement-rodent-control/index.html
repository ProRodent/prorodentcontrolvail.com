The garage as a rodent shelter.

A garage looks like part of the house, but it usually has fewer seals, more gaps and a lot of stored material. Cardboard, old fabric, paper and insulation are ideal nesting material, and a quiet corner behind shelving is a perfect nesting spot.

Many Vail homes use the garage for storage, a spare refrigerator or a workbench. Every one of those items adds something an animal wants: food, warmth from a motor, or soft material to shred.

Checks along the doors and walls.

  • Look at the bottom seal and both corners of the garage door for worn rubber or daylight.
  • Check the line where the wall meets the slab, especially behind shelving.
  • Inspect where wiring, plumbing and cable lines pass through the wall.
  • Look at the attic access, if there is one, and at any vent that opens to the outside.

A technician finds signs the same way you would, only with more practice. Droppings along a baseboard, gnawed packaging and rub marks near a gap all point to a route.

Lower levels and storage rooms.

If your home has a basement, a cellar or a below-grade storage room, the checks are the same: gaps, stored goods, pipes and wiring. Lower levels can stay cool and undisturbed, which is exactly what many animals look for.

A room that is rarely used is the most likely hiding place. If you have not been in it for a month, go in carefully with a flashlight and look at the floor along the walls before you start moving boxes.

Vehicles in the garage.

A vehicle parked in a garage can still be reached by animals that get in through the door gaps. Engine bays are warm and sheltered after a drive, and some animals use them as nests. Our vehicle and car rodent removal page covers that.

Closing the space.

Once animals are removed, the garage is sealed using metal, mesh and a better door seal, depending on what was found. Our exclusion service page explains the materials. If stored items were soiled, handle them with the wet-cleanup steps in the CDC pages linked here.

Handle with care: a CDC-based checklist

Read this list before you touch anything that looks like droppings, urine stains or nesting material.

  • Resist the urge to sweep. Dry droppings crumble into dust that is easy to inhale.
  • Before entering a closed space with signs of rodents, open it up and leave for about 30 minutes.
  • Wear rubber or plastic gloves for the whole job, and scrub your hands with soap and water at the end.
  • Use a disinfectant on the droppings and the surface under them, wait about five minutes, then wipe with disposable towels.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Garage and lower-level questions

Why do rodents like garages?

A garage offers shelter, stored cardboard and fabric for nests, and often pet food or birdseed. Gaps at the door and walls give easy access.

Does my home need to have a basement for this page to apply?

No. The same checks apply to a garage, a storage room, a casita or a basement or below-grade room if your home has one.

Can I just replace the door seal?

A new seal helps, but animals also use gaps in the walls and where lines pass through. The inspection checks all of them.

What should I do with stored boxes?

Lift cardboard off the floor and away from walls, and use sealed plastic tubs where you can. Do not shake out boxes that show droppings.

Is poison a good idea in a garage?

Poison can leave an animal dying inside a wall or behind stored items, and it puts pets at risk. Ask the technician before using any.
