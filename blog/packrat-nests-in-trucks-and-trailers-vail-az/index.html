Packrat Nests in Trucks and Trailers: A Vail Owner's Guide.

A pile of sticks in an engine bay, a chewed hose in a trailer, a smell from the vents. This guide covers what to check first and what can wait for a professional.

If you open a hood, a trailer panel or an access hatch and find a heap of sticks, plant stems and other debris, you are probably looking at a rodent nest. In the Southwest, a common builder is the packrat, also called a woodrat. This guide explains what to do next.

Do this first.

  • Do not start the engine if you see nest material or smell something burning.
  • Photograph the nest and any chewed wires or hoses before touching anything.
  • Keep children and pets away from the vehicle.
  • Wear gloves and avoid contact with droppings or nesting material.
  • Call someone who can handle both the animal and the parking spot.

A vehicle that sits for days or weeks is the most likely home. One driven every day is disturbed too often for most animals to settle in, although they may still visit at night.

Why vehicles appeal to packrats.

To a small animal, an engine bay is shelter with warm metal after a drive and many nooks. The space beside the engine, the air filter box and the area near the battery are dark and enclosed. Cabin air intakes near the windshield can be reached from outside as well.

Packrats collect material and build a large nest, called a midden, from sticks, cactus pieces and whatever else they find. Mice and rats also move into vehicles, and they build with finer material like shredded paper, insulation and fabric.

What the damage looks like.

Rodents gnaw constantly. In a vehicle that can mean wire insulation, hoses, sound-proofing and plastic fittings. Damage may appear as a warning light, a rough start or an odd smell, or it may only show when a mechanic looks.

Exposed wire can short and, beside dry debris, start a fire. Leave wiring repairs to an auto electrician or a mechanic, and ask a rodent service to deal with the animal and the place you park.

Cleaning a nest safely.

Nests and droppings can carry germs. The CDC advises against sweeping or vacuuming them dry. Air out the space, wear gloves, wet the material with disinfectant, wipe it up with paper towels and bag the waste. The steps appear in the safety box on this page.

If you do not want to handle it, ask a professional. Our vehicle and car rodent removal page explains how nests and parking areas are handled.

Parking, desert edges and a plan for next time.

Removing a nest is only half the job. If the vehicle goes back to the same spot, the animal can return. Vail is an unincorporated community in Pima County, and if you park near open desert, a woodpile or thick brush, mention it on the call so the technician checks the spot as well as the vehicle.

  • Is there food nearby, such as pet food, birdseed or spilled seed?
  • Is there brush, stacked material or a pile of boards beside the parking spot?
  • Does the garage have gaps at the door corners or the walls?
  • Does the vehicle sit unused for days or weeks at a time?

A technician can check the spot and close gaps. See garage and basement rodent control for the garage side of this, and look under the hood once a week if the vehicle sits.

What to ask a mechanic and a rodent service.

Two different jobs are involved after a nest in a vehicle, and they are done by two different kinds of business. Knowing which questions belong to whom keeps the repair from being repeated.

  • Ask the mechanic or auto electrician whether any wire, hose or sensor was chewed and whether it needs repair before the vehicle is driven.
  • Ask the rodent service where the animal came from and what gaps or habits let it reach the vehicle.
  • Ask whether the parking spot, garage or shed needs sealing or clearing.
  • Ask how soon to check the vehicle again, and what a returning nest would look like.

Keep any photos and notes from both. If a problem comes back, you can show what was found and fixed the first time.

When to call.

Call if you find a nest, see chewed wiring, smell burning or mustiness from the vents or hear scratching in a parked vehicle. Early is easier, because a nest found on day one is smaller than one that has had weeks to grow.

Steps to take before touching droppings or nests

General safety practice drawn from CDC guidance on rodents. It is meant to keep you safe before any professional arrives.

  • Leave the broom and vacuum in the closet. Dry cleanup of rodent waste sends particles into the air.
  • Ventilate a shut-up space first. Half an hour of airing out is the CDC's starting point.
  • Use disposable or washable rubber gloves, and wash both gloves and hands when you are done.
  • Dampen droppings and urine stains with disinfectant or diluted bleach, leave it for about five minutes, then wipe with paper towels.
  • Double check that waste is bagged and sealed, and never pick up a dead rodent with bare hands.
  • We cannot offer health advice. If symptoms appear after cleaning rodent waste, see a doctor and explain what you handled.

Reader questions

Can a nest start a vehicle fire?

It can contribute. Dry debris near hot parts and chewed wires are both concerns, so do not drive a vehicle with nest material or a burning smell until it has been checked.

Should I run the engine to see if it works?

Not if you can see nest material or smell something burning. Running the engine can overheat debris or send current through a damaged wire.

Will a mechanic remove the nest?

Some will. A rodent service also checks where the animal came from, closes the way in and reviews the parking spot.

Do mothballs, sprays or peppermint work?

None is a reliable deterrent, and some are hazardous to children and pets. Removing food, shelter and access works better.

Does this apply to trailers and RVs?

Yes. Parked trailers and recreational vehicles have the same warm, enclosed spaces and are checked the same way.
