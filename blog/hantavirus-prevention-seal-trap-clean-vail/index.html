Hantavirus and Rodent Droppings: Seal Up, Trap Up, Clean Up.

Hantavirus is rare, but the way it spreads is the reason cleanup rules exist. Here is the CDC's three-part approach in plain words.

Hantavirus gets attention because it can be serious, and because it is linked to something ordinary: rodent droppings in a shed, a garage or a cabin. The good news is that the CDC's prevention advice is practical and mostly common sense.

How it spreads, in plain terms.

According to the CDC, hantaviruses are carried by certain rodents, and people can become ill when they breathe in dust stirred up from contaminated droppings, urine or nesting material. That is why sweeping and vacuuming dry droppings is discouraged. It does not mean every mouse carries the virus, and it does not mean you should panic.

We are a pest service and not medical professionals. We cannot judge anyone's health risk, and we do not claim that any specific animal or home is infected. The point of this page is to explain why the cleanup steps are what they are.

Seal up.

Keeping rodents out is the most reliable way to avoid contact. Mice can pass through a gap about a quarter inch wide, and rats need roughly half an inch. Check pipe collars, vents, garage door corners and roof edges, and close gaps with metal, mesh and sealant.

Our exclusion and entry point sealing page lists the materials and the order of work.

Trap up.

Animals already inside need to be removed, and traps do that without spreading bait into the house. Traps go along the routes the evidence shows and are checked on a schedule. If you find a trapped or dead animal, use gloves and a sealed bag.

See rodent trapping and removal for how placement works and why more traps are not always better.

Clean up.

Cleanup is where the exposure risk is greatest, and it is where the wet method matters most. Air out a closed space, wear gloves, wet droppings and nests with disinfectant, wait about five minutes, wipe with paper towels and bag the waste. Wash your hands carefully afterward.

Those closed-up spaces are where droppings tend to build, so air them out before you go in. If you find a lot of droppings or a large nest, ask a professional to handle it.

Questions to ask before you enter a closed-up space.

Sheds, trailers and garages that have been shut for weeks are where people most often disturb droppings without realizing it. A short pause before you open the door lowers the risk and costs nothing but a few minutes.

  • How long has the space been closed, and has anything been stored in it that rodents could nest in?
  • Can you open the door or a window and let it air out for at least half an hour before you go in?
  • Do you have gloves, disinfectant, paper towels and sealable bags on hand?
  • Is the job small enough for you, or does it need a professional with proper gear?

If the answers point to a large, hot or hard-to-reach space, hand it off. The CDC approach is about avoiding raised dust, and the easiest way to do that is to keep the work wet and the space ventilated.

Putting the three steps together.

Seal, trap and clean work as a set. Skipping the first leaves new animals coming in, and skipping the last leaves old waste behind. A visit that begins with an inspection sorts out which step you need first.

For a deeper look at the cleanup method, read our guide to the safe cleanup of droppings at home. The safety box on this page repeats the core CDC points in a short list.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

Short, practical safety points based on CDC cleanup guidance. They are not medical advice.

  • Dry sweeping is the mistake to avoid. It lifts fine dust from droppings and nests into the room.
  • A space that has been closed for weeks should air out for at least 30 minutes before you work in it.
  • Put on gloves before touching anything contaminated, then wash your hands thoroughly when you finish.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Waste goes into a sealed bag, not an open bin. Use gloves or a tool for any dead animal.
  • Flu-like symptoms or shortness of breath after rodent exposure are a reason to see a doctor and mention the cleanup. Health questions belong with a physician, not a pest company.

Short answers to common questions

What is hantavirus?

It is a group of viruses carried by certain rodents. People can become ill by breathing in dust contaminated with droppings, urine or saliva, according to the CDC.

Does every mouse carry it?

No. Only certain rodents carry hantaviruses, which is why we do not diagnose or make health claims. Treat all droppings and nests with the same care.

What is seal up, trap up, clean up?

It is the CDC's short summary of prevention: close entry points, trap rodents that are inside and clean up safely.

Should I be worried about a vehicle or shed?

Closed-up spaces deserve the same precautions. Air them out first and avoid raising dust.

What if I feel sick after cleaning?

See a doctor, mention the rodent exposure and the dates. We cannot assess anyone's health.
