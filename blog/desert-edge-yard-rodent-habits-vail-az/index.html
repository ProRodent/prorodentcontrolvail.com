A yard that meets open ground sits where wild and domestic habitat overlap. Here is how to read your yard in three zones and what to change in each.

A yard is not one place. Even a small lot has a boundary where it meets something else, a middle where you live and a strip along the house where things get tight. Rodents treat each of those parts differently, and so should you.

Reading the yard in three zones.

A simple way to plan is to split the yard into three zones. The first is the lot edge, where your property meets open ground, a wash or a neighbor's overgrown fence. The second is the middle, with plantings, a shed, a pool or a patio. The third is the strip against the house itself.

  • Edge zone: keep brush thin, check for burrows and watch for gnawed lines.
  • Middle zone: store pet food and fruit carefully and keep stacked material off the ground.
  • House zone: keep a clear strip along the walls and close every gap you find.

Most of the value comes from the third zone, because that is where an animal either finds a way in or does not.

Habits that make the biggest difference.

Three habits do most of the work: keep food stored, keep cover away from the house and keep a clear strip along the walls. None of them needs special tools, and all of them help long after a visit.

  • Store pet food and birdseed in sealed containers, and bring bowls in overnight.
  • Pick up fallen fruit and clear seed from under feeders.
  • Keep branches back from the roof by a few feet.
  • Raise woodpiles and pallets off the ground and away from the walls.

What not to do.

Do not fill burrows before they have been checked, because blocking them can push animals toward the house. Do not scatter loose poison, because it can reach pets and wildlife. Federal rules limit what consumers can buy, and the EPA page linked below explains why.

A seasonal routine for the three zones.

Rodent pressure changes through the year, so a yard routine works best when it follows the seasons. A few minutes each season is enough to catch changes early, and the same walk can cover all three zones.

  • Spring: check the lot edge for new burrows and trim anything that touched the roof over winter.
  • Summer: keep pet water and food tidy and pick up fallen fruit as soon as it drops.
  • Monsoon months: clear debris from the base of walls after storms and look for new holes.
  • Fall: store seed and feed in sealed bins, and check sheds before cooler weather sends animals looking for shelter.

Write the dates on your calendar and take a few photos each time. A burrow, a gnawed line or a pile of droppings is much easier to describe when you can compare it with a picture from three months ago.

Safe handling checklist for droppings and nests

The CDC publishes cleanup steps for homes with rodent droppings or nests. This list restates the main points in plain words.

  • Skip the broom and the vacuum on dry droppings. Stirring them up is how dust gets airborne.
  • Do not walk straight into a sealed storage area. Open it, leave, and come back after about 30 minutes.
  • Wear rubber or plastic gloves for the whole job, and scrub your hands with soap and water at the end.
  • Wet first, wipe second. Disinfectant needs about five minutes on the surface before you remove anything.
  • Seal everything you wipe up in a bag before it goes in the trash, and do not handle carcasses bare-handed.
  • A fever or trouble breathing after rodent cleanup deserves a call to your doctor, with the exposure mentioned up front.

More questions about this topic

Does living next to open desert mean I will have rodents?

Not necessarily. It means animals can approach more easily, so food storage, cover and sealing matter more.

Are bird feeders a problem?

Spilled seed feeds rodents and the feeder itself can be a climbing route. Pausing a feeder or adding a tray under it helps.

Should I trim plants near the house?

Yes where they touch the roof or press against walls. A clear strip along the house gives animals fewer places to hide.

What about a woodpile?

Raise it off the ground and move it a few feet from the house. A woodpile against a wall is shelter right at the entry.

What do burrows mean?

Holes near foundations and sheds show activity. Photograph them and have a technician look before you fill anything.
