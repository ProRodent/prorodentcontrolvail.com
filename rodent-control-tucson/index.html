What people call about.

Callers rarely start with the word rodent. They describe a sound in the ceiling after dark, droppings in a garage or pantry, a gnawed hose, a nest in an engine bay or a smell that gets worse on warm days.

  • Scratching or thumping overhead at night.
  • Droppings in a garage, shed or cabinet.
  • Chewed wiring, hoses or irrigation lines.
  • Sticks and debris stacked in a vehicle or under a trailer.
  • A smell with no visible source.

Each of these points to a different animal and a different plan. Our mouse control and rat control pages explain the usual differences, and an inspection sorts out which one you have.

Edges of property and open ground.

A city lot among streets and a lot that backs onto a wash or open desert do not face the same pressure. We do not guess which one you have. The technician asks, looks and says what the evidence supports, and any property that touches undeveloped ground gets the boundary checked first.

While you wait, walk the outside once from a safe distance and without disturbing anything:

  • Brush, stacked material or debris against walls and sheds.
  • Pet food, feeders and fallen fruit within reach of the house.
  • Gaps at the roof edge, vents and garage door corners.
  • Anything stored in a car or truck that sits for days.

You do not need to know whether it is a mouse, a rat or a packrat. Say what you found and we will sort it out together.

A short safety list before anyone cleans up

These steps follow CDC guidance on cleaning up after rodents. They are general safety practice for any household.

  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings or nests. That can put contaminated dust in the air you breathe.
  • A space that has been closed for weeks should air out for at least 30 minutes before you work in it.
  • Protect your hands with gloves, then wash up carefully even if the gloves look clean.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Waste goes into a sealed bag, not an open bin. Use gloves or a tool for any dead animal.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.
