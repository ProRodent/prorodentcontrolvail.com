When to start with an inspection.

Some calls begin with a clear picture, such as a mouse in a trap or a nest under a hood. Many begin with a sound, a smell or a few droppings and no clear idea of the cause. An inspection exists for the second kind of call.

It is also the right start when a property is changing hands, when a home has been empty for weeks, or when a previous treatment seems not to have held. In each case the question is the same: what is there, and how is it getting in?

What a walk-through covers.

The technician begins where you noticed the signs and works outward. The attic is checked for droppings, nests and soiled insulation, the garage for gaps and stored goods, and the yard for burrows, feeding spots and plants that touch the roof.

Local conditions the technician keeps in mind.

Vail is an unincorporated community in Pima County, and Colossal Cave Mountain Park and the Rincon Mountains District of Saguaro National Park lie close by. Wild habitat is near for many addresses. If your lot touches open ground, the inspection includes the boundary line, because that is where animals approach.

Heat matters as well. Summer attics can be dangerous for anyone working in them, so the technician plans attic access for the safest time of day. Tell us on the call if the hatch is hard to reach or if the space has limited headroom.

What you hear at the end.

At the end of the visit you get a plain explanation. It names the animal, shows the openings, says whether trapping, sealing, cleanup or repair is needed and gives a price for each part.

You decide what to book. Some people want trapping and sealing together, others want only sealing because they have already removed the animals, and a few want only the written findings. All three are fine.

What an inspection cannot promise.

An inspection cannot count the animals in a wall and cannot guarantee that none will ever return. It can show where evidence is strongest, which openings are in use and which parts of the house need attention first.

From there the usual next steps are rodent trapping and removal and exclusion and entry point sealing. If the findings point to a vehicle, a garage or a yard, the matching service pages explain those paths.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

Nothing here is specific to our service. It is general cleanup safety, summarized from CDC guidance.

  • Treat dry droppings as something to dampen first, not something to brush up.
  • Give the room time to ventilate. Thirty minutes with the doors open is the common recommendation.
  • Wear rubber or plastic gloves for the whole job, and scrub your hands with soap and water at the end.
  • Dampen droppings and urine stains with disinfectant or diluted bleach, leave it for about five minutes, then wipe with paper towels.
  • Dispose of wipes and droppings in a sealed plastic bag, and keep skin off dead animals.
  • Doctors, not pest companies, judge health risk. See one if fever, aches or shortness of breath follow a cleanup.

Inspection questions

What does the technician look at?

The attic, garage, yard, roofline and any room where you noticed signs. The goal is to find fresh activity, identify the animal and locate each opening.

How long does a visit take?

It depends on the size of the property and how much access there is. The technician can tell you a window when the visit is booked.

Do I need to be home?

Someone should be there to give access to the attic and garage. Tell us on the call if that will be you or someone else.

Will I get a written plan?

You get an explanation of what was found and what each step involves. Ask the technician for notes if you want them for your records.

Can an inspection find nothing?

Yes, and an honest finding of no active rodents is a useful result. The technician will say what evidence supports it and what to watch for.
