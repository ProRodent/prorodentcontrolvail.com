Why cleanup is a separate step.

Trapping and sealing deal with the animals. Cleanup deals with what they leave behind: droppings, urine stains, shredded nesting material and flattened, soiled insulation. These can carry germs, and they do not go away when the animals do.

Cleanup comes after sealing for a practical reason. An attic that animals can still enter will be soiled again. Sealing first means the work is done once.

The safety rules that shape the job.

The CDC says not to sweep or vacuum dry droppings or nests, because that sends contaminated dust into the air. The recommended method is to air out the space, wear gloves, wet the material with disinfectant and wipe it up with paper towels. The safety box on this page repeats the main steps.

An attic adds heat, poor footing and low light. Those hazards are as real as the droppings, and they are why many homeowners prefer to leave the job to someone with the right gear.

Summer attics and storage in Vail.

Attics in southern Arizona can reach dangerous temperatures in summer, and the monsoon months from mid-June through September bring humidity and heavy rain. Both change how and when cleanup should happen. Tell us if you know the attic is hard to enter, and we plan the visit around the coolest safe window.

If your property is close to open ground, such as the land near Colossal Cave Mountain Park or the Rincon Mountains District of Saguaro National Park, mention it on the call. A lot that borders open ground is one more reason to confirm that every opening is closed before cleanup starts.

What happens after cleanup.

Once the space is cleaned, it is aired out and checked again. If insulation was removed, replacement can follow. If wiring or ducts were chewed, rodent damage repair scopes that as its own step.

If you are not sure the animals are gone, start with rodent trapping and removal instead. Cleanup done too early only has to be repeated.

Before you sweep, spray or bag anything

The CDC publishes cleanup steps for homes with rodent droppings or nests. This list restates the main points in plain words.

  • Treat dry droppings as something to dampen first, not something to brush up.
  • Do not walk straight into a sealed storage area. Open it, leave, and come back after about 30 minutes.
  • Protect your hands with gloves, then wash up carefully even if the gloves look clean.
  • Use a disinfectant on the droppings and the surface under them, wait about five minutes, then wipe with disposable towels.
  • Dispose of wipes and droppings in a sealed plastic bag, and keep skin off dead animals.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Cleanup questions

Can I clean the attic myself?

You can, but the CDC steps are strict: air out the space, wear gloves and respiratory protection, and wet droppings before wiping. Many people prefer to hand it to a professional.

Does the insulation have to be replaced?

Sometimes. If it is heavily soiled or matted with urine and nesting material, removal and replacement is the safer path. The technician will show you what they found.

How hot is too hot to work in an attic?

Summer attics can be dangerous, so work is planned for cooler hours. The technician will tell you if a visit should wait.

What about a crawl space?

Low spaces have their own risks, including tight access and poor air. The technician explains the plan and what you should leave to a professional.

Will the smell go away?

Removing the source and the soiled material usually clears it. A lingering odor may mean a second source or a soaked material.
